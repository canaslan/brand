# Principles

## 1. Inquisitive
Examine the problem, opportunity, and assumptions behind the brief. Match the depth of inquiry to what is at stake.

## 2. Integrated
Product design extends beyond the interface. Branding, product management, engineering, marketing, and organisational realities must feed into the process, as well as being fed by the design.

## 3. Judgement-led Design
Designers' intuition is often underutilised and underrated. Not every decision needs to be validated through research or offloaded to users. Experience and professional judgement have their own value, and designers should be trusted to exercise them as long as they make the reasoning explicit, test consequential decisions, and revise when evidence challenges them.

## 4. Design-led Product Management
The discipline of Product Management is essential, but the Product Manager role is sometimes a symptom of a poorly designed organisational structure. In self-sufficient teams, Product Management should ideally be owned by true end-to-end Product Designers, because design is more than usability.

## 5. Competitive
Product design should be more than a response to customer problems or needs. It should strengthen the competitive position of the product and the business. Designers must understand their market, anticipate competitive threats, and identify opportunities to differentiate.

## 6. Future-conscious
Design must consider not only the problems of today, but the possibilities of tomorrow. Emerging technologies, changing behaviours, and shifting market conditions should inform what we build today, not merely how we react tomorrow.

## 7. Timeless
Design for longevity, not trends. The founding principles of the World Wide Web, including openness and accessibility, should guide what is built. Technologies and aesthetics may evolve, but good foundations should endure.

## 8. Calm
Technology should sit in the periphery until it's needed. Interfaces should inform without demanding focus. No element should fight for attention unless it has earned the right to interrupt.
